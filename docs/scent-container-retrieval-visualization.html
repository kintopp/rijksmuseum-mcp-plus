Retrieving Scent Containers

Why the Rijksmuseum's perfume and incense holdings are unreachable by subject index

Iconclass 41C761 — scent-box — returns nothing at the Rijksmuseum. Its sibling 41C762 — incense-burner — returns 157 works, but not a single physical incense burner: they are all pictures of one. The museum's actual scent containers live in a second, entirely separate index built from Dutch object-type vocabulary. This page measures how far apart the two indexes sit, and how wide each retrieval channel is.

1 · A dead branch of the subject tree

41C761 sits under 41C76 — scents, perfumes ~ stimulants, drugs, itself under 41C7 — drinks, stimulants, drugs. Walking the immediate neighbourhood shows the notation is not merely under-used: the whole 41C76 branch is empty at the Rijksmuseum except for one sibling, which carries almost the entire signal.

The related notation 31A51451 (pomander, musk ball) sits in an unrelated branch — human figure and adornment, not nutrition and stimulants — and returns 4 works. So the collection's pomanders and its incense burners are indexed under two subject trees that never meet, and its scent-boxes under neither.

2 · Depictions and objects are disjoint sets

The 157 works under 41C762 are overwhelmingly prints. The physical incense burners, kogo and pomanders are reachable only through object-type vocabulary. Intersecting the subject index against every object-type channel returns zero every time — these are not overlapping views of one corpus, they are two corpora.

Share of each corpus by century of production. Depictions cluster hard in the 18th century (Japanese printmaking, largely); the objects themselves stretch back to Han-dynasty and Song-dynasty ceramics that no print records.

4 · What the type field misses

The clearest measure of cataloguing coverage is to run the same Dutch word through the controlled type field and through free-text description. For wierookbrander the two disagree about four times out of five.

Objects hidden behind form-words

The 112 works the type field misses are largely catalogued by physical form rather than function — hibachi, lid, box — or as components of a parent object. Semantic search surfaces them because it reads the description, not the type.

The one scent-box isn't typed as one

The single work whose title is literally Reukdoos carries the object type miniature. type: reukdoos returns zero — the label does not exist in the type vocabulary. Its three Iconclass codes describe the scenes chased into the silver, not the vessel.

One type label is fully contained in another

All 12 works typed kogo are also typed wierookdoos — a strict subset, not an overlap. Querying kogo after wierookdoos adds nothing; the reverse adds 8. Where two labels nest like this, only the broader one is worth issuing.

type: kogo → 12  ⊂  type: wierookdoos → 20   |   union = 20, added by kogo = 0

5 · Every English type term returns zero

Object-type vocabulary in this collection is Dutch. Seven plausible English labels for scent containers return nothing at all; their Dutch equivalents return the whole corpus. This is not a ranking penalty — it is an empty result set, indistinguishable from “the museum owns none.”

Three of the seven zero-results are Dutch words — reukdoos, geurdoos, reukwater. Speaking the right language is necessary but not sufficient: the term also has to have been adopted into the type authority. The working labels are a mix of Dutch (wierookbrander, reukfles, reukbal), Japanese loanwords (kogo, hibachi), and French-derived Dutch (parfumbrander).

Reading this as a retrieval strategy. For this material class the subject index is not a weak handle, it is the wrong handle: it answers “what is pictured” when the question is “what is held.” Object-type vocabulary answers the right question but only in Dutch, only for labels that made it into the authority, and it still misses roughly three in four works that the cataloguer described as incense burners. The widest single channel is free-text Dutch description; the most complete is the union of all three, plus semantic search to catch objects filed under form-words like lid and box.
